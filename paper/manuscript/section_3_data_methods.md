# 3. Data and Methods

## 3.1 Data Sources and Collection

The empirical backbone of this study is the Spanish-language Wikipedia (*es.wikipedia.org*), treated as a large, publicly edited prosopographical archive rather than a census of real-world elites. Biographical entries were harvested through a structured scrape that combined surname- and family-themed articles with category-based lists of notable persons, stratified by the ten republics under comparison: Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Mexico, Paraguay, Peru, Uruguay, and Venezuela. For each individual page, machine-readable infobox fields were extracted wherever present, including personal name, attributed family (*familia*), country, birth and death dates, places of birth and death, occupation, nationality, spouse, parents, children, partner, siblings, political party, education, and predecessor–successor links in political office. The opening paragraph of the article (*biografia_inicial*) and the full biography text were retained to support secondary detection of cross-national mentions. Kinship fields frequently embed hyperlinks to related biographies in the form "Name (URL)"; these URLs provide the primary keys used to resolve relatives across pages.

Raw extracts were consolidated in `00_consolidar_familias.R`, producing `_CONSOLIDADO_familias_latam.csv` (semicolon-delimited, one row per scraped profile). Subsequent normalization in `02_leer_data.R` yields relational tables stored as RDS objects: `personas` (the master person table), `relaciones` (kinship edges), `partidos` and `educacion` (person-by-affiliation), `sucesiones` (office succession), and a derived `grafo` object for network analysis. After deduplication and cleaning, the master person table contains **[CHECK: N = 6,169]** individuals with non-missing identifiers; **[CHECK: 5,732 (92.9%)]** of these have a parsed birth year (`anio_nacimiento`), anchoring descriptive temporal analyses to individual lifecourses rather than to edges. The kinship table comprises **[CHECK: 14,190]** directed relation rows extracted from infoboxes; of these, **[CHECK: 8,201 (57.8%)]** were matched to another scraped individual via `persona_relacionada_id`, constituting the core empirical set of *documented* inter-person ties. The remainder are retained as text-only stubs and are excluded from matched-edge statistics. Manual codings in `data/manual/url_pais_extra.csv` correct misassigned country labels for specific URLs, and `data/manual/conexiones_familiares_manuales.csv` supplements cross-border ties that the automated parse under-recovers. These interventions follow the spirit of careful linkage work in historical network studies (Padgett & Ansell, 1993) while remaining transparently bounded in scope.

## 3.2 Network Construction

Nodes are defined at the level of the individual Wikipedia biography: each retained profile receives a unique `persona_id` and carries attributes for normalized family label (`familia_norm`), effective country (`pais_efectivo` / `pais_base` in the pipeline), life dates, occupation string, and—where extracted—party and educational affiliation. Edges are organized into three substantive layers, reflecting distinct evidentiary channels.

*Layer 1 — Kinship (primary).* Spouses, parents, children, and unmarried partners are coded from infobox kinship fields. Directionality in the raw table is retained for bookkeeping, but analytical representations are undirected: marriage and descent are treated as symmetric ties for centrality and community detection. Partners are matched first by Wikipedia URL, then by exact string equality on normalized names, then by conservative partial matching when a URL is absent. As noted above, **[CHECK: 8,201]** relations link two scraped individuals; these are the backbone for endogamy calculations and cross-border kinship counts.

*Layer 2 — Diplomatic and biographical cross-national signals (secondary).* Additional person-by-country associations are inferred when country-specific keyword lists—constructed separately for each republic—appear in death place, residence, birth place, political office strings, occupation, or the lead paragraph. The resulting pairs feed biographical “mention” weights in country–country aggregates (e.g., `n_biografico` in `transnacional_red_paises.csv`), distinct from strictly genealogical bridges.

*Layer 3 — Institutional affiliation (tertiary).* Shared party membership and shared educational institution generate person–person edges through bipartite projection: two individuals are linked if they list the same party or the same university. These ties are used in institutional visualizations and robustness checks rather than as substitutes for kinship.

Country assignment follows a fixed priority: manual overrides, then inference from nationality, birthplace, and lead text, then the country associated with the crawl list (*pais_base*). Family assignment relies on Wikipedia’s *familia* field, lowercased and trimmed, with missing values mapped to a “no family” category—an imperfect but reproducible rule that mirrors how surname clusters are mobilized in regional elite historiography (Balmori, Voss, & Wortman, 1984), without claiming genealogical certification.

For **global centrality, PageRank, Louvain communities, and clustering**, the operational graph in `03_grafo_parentesco.R` **augments** matched kinship edges with (i) within-family edges among members of small lineages (≤15 persons) to limit isolated clusters, (ii) predecessor–successor edges from `sucesiones`, and (iii) projected same-party and same-university ties. The resulting undirected multiplex graph contains **[CHECK: V = 6,169]** nodes and **[CHECK: E ≈ 2.1 × 10^5]** edges after union; kinship-only subgraphs remain available for hypothesis tests that require interpretive purity. ## 3.3 Variables and Operationalization

**Network metrics (individual level).** Using `igraph` in R, undirected summaries were computed on the augmented graph: *degree* counts adjacent edges; *betweenness* measures the share of shortest paths on which a node lies, indexing potential brokerage; *PageRank* provides a recursive prestige score; the *local clustering coefficient* captures triadic closure in each ego network; and *Louvain modularity* (resolution 1.0) partitions nodes into communities. [TABLE 2] should excerpt `metricas_centralidad.csv` (top brokers by country). Illustrative names with extreme betweenness in the current extract include Luis Felipe Villarán and Walter Humala (Peru), José Evaristo Uriburu (Argentina), and Cuauhtémoc Cárdenas (Mexico)—reported here only as *examples* of structural positions, not as substantive historical claims.

**Endogamy (family level).** Following standard practice in kinship studies, a marriage or partnership edge is *endogamous* when `familia_norm` matches on both endpoints; *transnational* when `pais_efectivo` differs. Country-level rates are computed as endogamous unions divided by unions with a matched partner. [TABLE 3] draws on `endogamia_por_pais.csv`; in the present extract, national endogamy rates range from **[CHECK: 0.67 (Bolivia) to 1.00 (Paraguay, n=4 marriages)]**, with large countries clustering near **[CHECK: 0.77–0.87]**. These magnitudes feed **H1**, which contrasts marital closure with political concentration (Herfindahl indices of party shares) at the family and country levels (`T02_h1_h4_resumen_pais.csv`).

**Transnational reach (family and country levels).** Cross-border kinship edges are flagged when endpoints’ effective countries differ; pairs are aggregated into a directed country matrix (`transnacional_red_paises.csv`). The strongest weighted pairs in the current data are **[CHECK: Chile–Peru (n_total = 117), Argentina–Uruguay (105), Argentina–Chile (94), Argentina–Peru (90)]**, combining kinship and biographical signals as reported in the table. At the family level, `transnacional_familias_multi.csv` ranks lineages by the number of distinct countries represented: **[CHECK: Borrero, Campero, Ponce de León, and Sucre span four countries each; Pinto (Chile) counts 50 members across three countries]**. These statistics motivate **H3** (Pacific–Andean corridors) and **H4** (brokerage versus geographic reach).

**Dynastic persistence.** For each `familia_norm`, temporal spread is measured as the difference between maximum and minimum birth years among members; internal succession in office is coded when predecessor and successor share a family label (`05_dinastias_temporales.R`).

**Macro topology (qualitative).** Country subgraphs were inspected with Fruchterman–Reingold layouts and degree summaries. Ideal types—oligarchic web, dynastic star, hereditary chain, fragmented cluster—are **interpretive labels** assigned after visual and distributional review, in dialogue with comparative state-formation narratives (Centeno, 2002), not output of a classifier. **Hypothesis mapping.** **H1** pairs family-level closure indices with party- and office-concentration metrics (`T01_h1_h4_familias_pais.csv`, `T02_*`). Country-level summaries already exhibit negative correlations between genealogical degree and closure in several polities—for example **[CHECK: Argentina r(degree, closure) ≈ −0.74, Mexico ≈ −0.80, Peru ≈ −0.72 from `T02_h1_h4_resumen_pais.csv`]**—which we treat as *structuring expectations* for the narrative rather than as definitive tests. **H2** aligns qualitative topology with historical regime sketches. **H3** invites models of country-pair tie counts as functions of shared colonial history and geographic separation. **Distance** is operationalized as **[CHECK: great-circle kilometers between national capitals (or population-weighted centroids); matrix to be joined to `transnacional_red_paises.csv`)]**; alternative specifications may substitute contiguity dummies or shortest maritime path to probe robustness to the Andean-Pacific corridor hypothesis. **H4** contrasts individual or family betweenness with transnational reach (number of countries spanned or edge-weight to foreign nodes). ## 3.4 Analytical Strategy

Analysis proceeds at three scales. **Micro-level** descriptions profile degree, betweenness, and community membership within and across countries; brokers are identified from the upper tails of `metricas_centralidad.csv`, with gendered patterns explored using name-based gender inference where reported. **Meso-level** aggregation groups individuals by `familia_norm` to compute endogamy rates, transnational breadth, and temporal depth, and constructs family–party bipartite projections for institutional concentration. Families that combine **high** transnational dispersion with **high** intermarriage closure (e.g., large multi-country lineages such as Borrero or Campero in `transnacional_familias_multi.csv`) illustrate how “reach” and “closure” are analytically distinct dimensions of elite structure. **Macro-level** work compares country-pair matrices to gravity-style expectations. A baseline specification—**[CHECK: to be estimated]**—takes the form

$$\log(1 + n_{ij}) \sim \log(\text{distance}_{ij}) + \log(N_i N_j) + \mathbf{1}\{\text{shared colonial orbit}\} + \varepsilon_{ij},$$

where $n_{ij}$ is the aggregate tie count (kinship plus biographical signals, depending on the table row), $N_i$ and $N_j$ are corpus sizes or elite counts for countries $i$ and $j$, and distance enters as a test of whether documented ties decay with geographic separation after conditioning on mass. Standard errors should cluster by country or use permutation schemes **[CHECK: inferential protocol]**; until such models are run, we report raw rankings and visual evidence only [TABLE 5]. Software consists of **R 4.x** with `igraph`, `tidygraph`, `ggraph`, `ggplot2`, `patchwork`, `ggrepel`, `rnaturalearth`, `sf`, and core `tidyverse` tools.

All inferential claims in the main text remain **descriptive and associational**: correlations and visual patterns are used to structure narrative comparison, not to secure causal identification. The limitations subsection makes this epistemic stance explicit.

## 3.5 Limitations and Bias

Several biases are structural rather than incidental. **First, Wikipedia selection** over-represents politically visible, Spanish-literate, nationally prominent, and twentieth-century figures; indigenous and Afro-descendant elites, regional strongmen with thin digital footprints, women (especially before 1900), and economically powerful families without political infobox cues are systematically under-counted. **Second, family labels are editor-generated**: `familia_norm` may split or merge lineages in ways no archival genealogy would endorse; compound surnames amplify noise in cross-country matching. **Third, temporal conflation**: edges are not time-stamped; ties between nineteenth-century ancestors and late-twentieth-century descendants enter the same static graph, although **[CHECK: 92.9%]** of nodes carry a birth year for cohort diagnostics. **Fourth, edge incompleteness**: only documented infobox kinship is captured; **[CHECK: 42.2%]** of raw relation rows lack a matched endpoint, and covert business or patronage ties are invisible by design. **Fifth, single-country assignment** forces multinational biographies into one `pais_efectivo`, partially mitigated by manual overrides and biographical mention layers. **Sixth, causality**: network position correlates with—but does not uniquely determine—political outcomes; historical counterfactuals lie outside the scope of this corpus.

Transparency about these limits frames the contribution as a **mapped documentary elite**—a digital sibling to classical *notables* research (Balmori et al., 1984)—rather than as an exhaustive social register. Subsequent empirical sections interpret magnitudes accordingly.
