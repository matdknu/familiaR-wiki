\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}\quad\small
How do elite family networks structure the reproduction of political power in Latin America across the long nineteenth to twenty-first centuries? Using genealogical data scraped from Spanish-language Wikipedia for 6,169 notable individuals across ten countries, we construct kinship networks based on spouse, parent--child, and partner ties (8,201 matched edges between identified individuals). We combine network topology measures---degree, betweenness, PageRank, and Louvain community detection---with country-level aggregates of endogamy, transnational reach, and dynasty persistence. Descriptive and correlational analyses speak to five hypotheses. H1--H4 address surface features of the network: (H1) family closure covaries with political concentration; (H2) macro-scale network topology (chain, star, web, fragment) aligns with stylized regime types; (H3) transnational family ties follow Pacific--Andean rather than Atlantic corridors in the aggregate; (H4) betweenness centrality identifies individuals in structural brokerage positions potentially relevant to cross-national influence. An integrative fifth hypothesis (H5) asks whether these surface patterns share a generative substrate. Applying an exponential random graph model, a stochastic block model, a homophily decomposition, a cohort-level temporal analysis, a targeted-attack robustness simulation, and a Gould--Fernandez brokerage decomposition, we document a radically asymmetric core--periphery architecture (21 families, 16 of them Chilean, with an internal tie probability 208$\times$ that of the periphery); a monotonic 70\% decline in the cross-family tie share from the Colonial to the Modern cohort; and a set of four Medici-like robust brokers---Caicedo (Colombia), and Cruz, Pinto, and Vicuña (Chile)---whose removal fragments the graph. Six of the ten most critical families under targeted attack are core members, a convergence between a generative and an interventional method. We find substantial heterogeneity across republics on surface features, but a single core--periphery architecture beneath them. The strongest cross-national weights in the documented graph link Chile--Peru and Argentina--Uruguay. We emphasize that Wikipedia encodes a \emph{documentary} elite biased toward literate, nationally visible strata. Causal claims are hedged throughout; findings are best interpreted as structured description of a collective digital archive whose documentary elite exhibits a single core--periphery architecture, rather than as an exhaustive map of Latin American elites.
